## 1. The short version

You gave us the FTC's COPPA Rule, and we are building BookBuddies to meet it from the start. That protects you whichever way the project goes. The rule has no size limit. It does not matter whether five children use the app or five thousand. What matters is whether an organization that is covered by the rule collects personal information from children under 13.

While BookBuddies stays a small group, or just you, the day-to-day exposure is low and our design handles most of what the software can handle. The picture changes when the app is released publicly, when other people's children join, when child data goes to outside companies, when money is involved, or when someone other than you and the team has to run it. At that point a good deal of what is needed is not software at all. Someone has to be the legally responsible organization, someone has to write and publish the legal documents, and someone has to answer parents and keep the security work going year after year.

## 2. Who has to write the legal documents

This is the most important point in this briefing, so we put it first. **The privacy policy, the COPPA compliance policy, the data retention policy, the terms of use, and the parent consent wording must come from you, with the help of a lawyer.** The team cannot write them. We are students and software developers, and a privacy policy is a legal statement the responsible organization makes to the public. If it is wrong, or if it says something the app does not actually do, the organization that published it carries the consequences, and we are not in a position to carry or to judge them.

What the team can do is supply the facts a lawyer will need. We can give an accurate technical description of what the app collects from a parent and from a child, why each item is collected, where it is stored, who can see it, which outside services touch it, and how it is deleted. We can build the places in the app where these documents will appear and link to them: the notice a parent sees before consenting, the privacy link on the first screen and next to every place a child is asked for information, and the parent's tools to review and delete their child's information. Until the documents exist, the app will show clearly marked placeholders, and nothing in the app should be described as "COPPA compliant."

If BookBuddies stays a small private project, you can decide how much of this is needed. If you intend to release it to a broader audience, please plan to hire or consult a lawyer with children's-privacy experience before the first outside family signs up. Section 8 says who to ask.

## 3. What COPPA is, in plain terms

COPPA is a U.S. federal law enforced by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) through a rule, 16 CFR 312. The text you sent us is saved in the team's repository as `docs/coppa/coppa-full` (https://github.com/videogames21/book_buddies) and is also published at https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule. It applies to websites and apps directed to children under 13, which BookBuddies is, with its audience of ages 6 to 11, and that collect personal information from them.

In everyday terms, the rule asks a covered organization to do the following. Before it collects anything from a child, it must get verifiable permission from the child's parent, meaning a method that reasonably confirms the person is the parent, not just a box ticked by anyone. It must tell parents clearly what is collected, how it is used and who receives it, and it must post a privacy notice at the front of the app and wherever information is requested. A parent must be able to see what is held about their child, stop further collection, and have it deleted, at any time. The organization may collect only what is reasonably necessary for what the child is doing, even if the parent has said yes to more. It may keep the information only as long as needed, and it must have a written retention policy with a deletion timeframe, never keeping data indefinitely. It must keep a written security program and make sure any outside service that holds the data will protect it. And it must get a separate parental yes before giving a child's data to a third party for anything that is not essential to running the service. The FTC says that disclosure for money, for advertising, or to train AI is never treated as essential. A provider that only helps run the service, a hosting company for example, is not counted as a third party as long as it uses the data for nothing else, but the organization must first get that provider's written assurance that it will keep the data secure.

"Personal information" is broader than a name. The rule's list includes identifiers that recognize a user over time, such as cookie IDs, IP addresses and device IDs, as well as photos and voice recordings, and any other information collected from a child that is combined with one of those identifiers. The rule text does not make clear whether a BookBuddies account ID on its own would count. To be safe, the team treats quiz answers, shelf, ratings and reflections on a child's account as personal information.

## 4. Where you are now and where you might go

Today the project is probably at one of four stages, and what COPPA asks of you grows with each.

If it is just you and the team, testing with adult stand-ins, no real child is using the app and almost nothing is being collected from children, so there is little exposure. This is also the cheapest time to settle design choices.

If a small group of real children you know takes part, perhaps as a pilot or a study, the rule still applies when the organization behind the app is covered. Each child's parent has to give verifiable consent, and the notice, review, delete and retention rules apply from the first child. "Small and friendly" is not an exemption. If the project turns out not to be covered, it is still sensible to follow the same steps, because you asked for COPPA to be built in and because it is far cheaper to do this now than to add it later. If the pilot is also research, an ethics review is likely as well (section 6).

If the app is released publicly, everything above still holds, and real-world operation begins. Parent requests arrive from strangers, security incidents become possible, and regulators and app stores can notice you. A named organization must appear in the privacy notice with real contact details.

If the project grows into funding, charging, advertising, partnerships with schools, or a company taking it over, the legally responsible "operator" becomes a business, outside vendors multiply, the data becomes more valuable, and additional laws apply.

## 5. What it takes to run it, not just build it

Software handles part of this, and the rest is ongoing work by people. Somebody has to answer parent requests, which means confirming the requester really is the child's parent, showing them the data, and deleting it on request. Somebody has to run the security program the rule requires: a named person in charge, a risk assessment at least once a year, testing, and an annual review. Somebody has to collect written security assurances from the hosting company, the email service and any analytics or AI provider before they hold child data. Somebody has to keep the notices up to date and ask parents to consent again whenever what is collected, how it is used, or who receives it changes in a material way. Somebody has to make sure deletion actually happens on schedule under the written retention policy. The design will keep a record of each parent's consent, with the date, the method and the version of the notice shown. The rule does not list record keeping by name, but a record is how you show that consent was obtained. Finally, someone should know what to do if the data is ever exposed. That is not a COPPA requirement as such, but state breach laws and ordinary good practice call for it.

At the moment none of these has an owner once the team graduates. That is the single biggest gap between a project that works for a small group and one that can be released more widely.

## 6. Legal topics to research

Please treat the rest of this section as a reading list for a lawyer, not as conclusions.

Start with the COPPA Rule itself, and in particular its definition of "operator." The rule covers commercial services and excludes some nonprofits, and whether a project like BookBuddies counts depends on who runs it and how, which is a question for a lawyer. Related to that, ask what penalties exist and who can enforce them. We believe the FTC can seek civil penalties for each violation, at more than $50,000 each at last check and adjusted every year, and that state attorneys general can also enforce COPPA. Neither the amount nor state enforcement appears in the rule text we were given, which says only that a violation is treated as an unfair or deceptive practice under the FTC Act, so please **verify** both with a lawyer and ask how enforcement is likely to apply to a project like this.

Next, look at state laws. Many states have their own privacy laws, some with extra protections for minors, design standards for children's services, or age-verification rules. They differ and change often, so start with California and Texas and any state where your users live, and **verify** with counsel. Every state also has a data-breach notification law that says who must be told, and how quickly, if personal data is exposed.

If BookBuddies will collect data from children for research, human-subjects rules, in particular the federal Common Rule with its added protections for children, and review by an ethics board (an IRB) may apply on top of COPPA. An IRB would want its own consent and assent process, and a lawyer or research-ethics adviser can tell you which board could review the project. This connects to `OI-13`, which asks whether real children can be used for testing.

Schools and teachers are a separate topic. The teacher role was cut from the project, so this is dormant, but if schools return, the federal student-records law (FERPA) and school-contract rules come into play. The FTC proposed a "school authorization" exception to parental consent but did not finalize it, to avoid conflicting with expected changes to the FERPA regulations. It says it will keep enforcing COPPA in education settings under its existing guidance, which is not part of the rule text we were given. So the rule text does not settle whether a school can consent in place of a parent. Our design has parents consent and teachers only flag, and a lawyer should tell you what school use would need.

Your notes have the platform report violent or self-harm content to parents and to "authorities," and keep a child's real name so the platform can report to law enforcement. Who counts as an authority, whether the platform has a legal duty to report, and what it may lawfully release all vary by state and need legal advice (`OI-18`, `OI-23`). The COPPA Rule's exceptions on this point (§ 312.5(c)(5)–(6)) let an operator collect a child's name and contact details, without prior parental consent, to protect a child's safety, respond to legal process, or help law enforcement with a public-safety matter, and only for that purpose. They do not say that a platform may release stored child data to authorities, so that question also needs legal advice.

Ownership and licensing need to be settled in writing: who owns the code and the data (you, the individual team members, or a company formed for the project). Separately, the book dataset you plan to use (`OI-1`, Kaggle or GitHub) comes with its own licence, and book covers and descriptions carry copyright, so check what the licence allows and whether it permits commercial use. If a mobile version follows the web version (`OI-10`), Apple and Google each have child-specific app rules that go beyond COPPA, which you should **verify** before building. If you ever message parents by text, telephone-marketing law applies as well, though we plan email only. Last, ask about cyber-liability insurance that covers children's data.

## 7. How the open issues change as the project grows

These come from `docs/requirements/OPEN-ISSUES.md`. Each is easy to put off in a small pilot and costly to leave open later.

The first group is about who is responsible. `OI-20` asks whether COPPA legally applies to the project and who the operator is. While the project is small the team simply builds as if it applies, but if it goes further this becomes the central question, because the operator signs the notices, answers parents and carries the legal duty, and it needs a lawyer to answer. `OI-9` asks who hosts and maintains BookBuddies after graduation and on what budget. It is not urgent today, but someone must eventually own security, retention, vendor contracts and parent requests, and hosting and security are recurring costs.

The second group is about consent and sharing. `OI-21` asks which method of parental consent the app uses. For a small group, an email with a confirming second step is enough and is the cheapest. That method is valid only while the service does not "disclose" child data, meaning release it to third parties other than providers that only help run the service, or make it public. If sharing between children, advertising, or an outside AI vendor that uses the data for its own purposes are added, a stronger method is needed, such as a payment check, an ID check, knowledge questions, or a video or phone call, which costs money and adds friction for parents. `OI-2`, whether the first version needs AI recommendations, belongs here too. Recommendation built into the app keeps all data in-house and is the simplest path. An outside AI vendor that uses child data for its own purposes, or trains a model on it, is a third party and needs separate parental consent. A vendor that only runs our recommendations under written security assurances may count as helping run the service, since the rule lists personalizing content among those functions, but that is a legal call. `OI-19`, how violent or self-harm content in reflections is detected, is a similar matter: a keyword list or manual review keeps a child's writing in-house, while an outside moderation service receives it and must have written security assurances and may not use the text for anything else. Detection mistakes are also a safety and liability matter.

The third group is about what data is kept and who can see it. `OI-23` asks whether the child's real name is needed at all and for how long. Real names are among the most sensitive data in the system, and every item held raises security duties and the consequences of a breach, so keeping less is the safer choice. `OI-22` asks how long data is kept and how long a parent has to confirm consent. The rule requires a written, published retention policy and an automatic deletion process, but it does not set the number of days or months. `OI-15` asks what the admin may see about a child. Every person with access to child data is a risk, so this needs access controls, logging and a reviewed list of who is allowed. `OI-12` and `OI-11`, expected numbers of users and how success is measured, matter because more users mean more parent requests and more data to secure, and success measures such as engagement need data about children, so each measure should be justified rather than collected by default.

The last group is a handful of separate items. `OI-13` asks whether real child testers can be reached and what consent applies, and for research use that likely means ethics approval and the same retention and deletion rules for the testers' data. `OI-1`, the source of book data, is a one-time dataset import for a small group, but for wider release you must check the licence, and a live book-data service must never be sent anything tied to a child. `OI-16`, whether the 24-hour parent review stays, is not a COPPA consent mechanism; if it stays, the system must enforce it on the server, and a parent who never logs in leaves the child's account running. `OI-10`, web first and mobile later, matters because app stores add their own child-safety rules and review.

## 8. Who to contact

A lawyer with children's-privacy or technology experience, ideally with COPPA and FTC experience, is the first call. They can answer the questions in section 9, review the notices, and tell you whether the project is a covered operator. Please see them before any real child uses the app, and certainly before public release.

The same lawyer, or a business attorney, can also help with the business side: whether to form a company to be the operator, who owns the code, and written agreements between you and the people who build and run the app. That is where `OI-20` and `OI-9` start. If the app collects research data from children, a lawyer or research-ethics adviser can say which review board, such as an independent IRB, should approve the ethics and consent forms before any child participates.

As you head toward public release, consider an FTC-approved COPPA Safe Harbor program. The rule text names PRIVO, kidSAFE, ESRB and CARU. These programs review and certify compliance, joining is optional, and following a program's approved guidelines counts as compliance with the rule. A security professional can do the independent risk assessment and testing that the security program calls for, before launch and then each year, and a cyber-liability insurance broker can advise on coverage once the user base grows beyond a controlled pilot. The FTC also publishes free plain-language COPPA guidance on ftc.gov, which is good background reading at any time.

## 11. What the team is doing in the meantime

We are building to the COPPA Rule from the start (see `docs/coppa/coppa-design-requirements.md`), which in short means a parent account and verified consent before any child data is collected, no release of child data to third parties, collecting only what is needed, a retention schedule, and a security plan.
